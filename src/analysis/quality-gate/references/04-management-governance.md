# Management, Culture, and Corporate Governance

> Trimmed from buffett-skills (github.com/agi-now/buffett-skills) reference 04-management-governance.

## Three-Dimensional Management Evaluation

**1. Integrity (one-strike disqualifier).** Not just "not lying" — proactively confronting bad news. Test: is this person willing to tell you what they don't know? Red flags: reports full of optimistic language with failures buried in footnotes, habitual "adjusted" metrics masking real issues, mistakes never acknowledged, frequent non-transparent related-party transactions. A CEO who misleads in public eventually misleads himself in private.

**2. Capital allocation ability.** Most CEOs are promoted for other skills and face capital allocation unprepared. Judge the 10-year record: M&A, buybacks, dividends, reinvestment — did value get created? Buybacks only below intrinsic value? Any overpaid "do a deal" acquisitions? Empire-building impulse?

**3. Owner mentality.** The most prized trait: managing the business as if it were the family's sole asset. Concrete signals: focus on per-share intrinsic value rather than headline EPS; willingness to close value-destroying businesses even if the company shrinks; personal wealth tied to held shares, not options.

## Institutional Imperative

An invisible organizational pressure that makes managers do what a rational actor would not: herd expansion (there's always a reason to acquire), size worship (never contract or divest), peer-comparison traps (everyone is doing it), busyness bias (waiting is the hardest correct decision). Watch for it: serial expensive acquisitions, peer-benchmarked compensation, a CEO under "pressure to deploy cash."

## Culture

Culture is the collective habit of decisions made when no one is watching — the hardest advantage to replicate. Good signs: low turnover, internal promotion, decentralized decision-making, bad news travels upward fast. Bad signs: senior-management churn, sales culture over product quality, politicized resource allocation, successes-only reporting.

## Acquisition Discipline

Berkshire's criteria: meaningful size; consistent demonstrated earning power (no turnaround projections); good returns on equity with little debt; honest management; simple understandable business; sensible asking price. "It's far better to buy a wonderful company at a fair price than a fair company at a wonderful price."

Common pitfalls: optimistic synergy assumptions, premium paid for empire building, underestimated integration costs, key-management departure after purchase.

## Governance Checklist

- Does the board genuinely evaluate the CEO? (Real independence is directors with their own money in the stock, not board-fee professionals.)
- Does management explain "why," not just "what"?
- Is compensation aligned via held shares rather than options?
- Is bad news disclosed proactively, in plain language?
