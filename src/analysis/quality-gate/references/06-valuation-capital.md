# Valuation, Capital Allocation, and Margin of Safety

> Trimmed from buffett-skills (github.com/agi-now/buffett-skills) reference 06-valuation-capital.

## Intrinsic Value: Three Methods

**1. Owner-earnings multiple (most common; stable, predictable businesses).**
Intrinsic value ≈ normalized owner earnings × appropriate multiple. Normalize over 3–5 years, excluding one-time items.

| Business characteristics | Multiple |
|--------------------------|---------|
| Wide moat + 5%+ growth + asset-light | 20–25x |
| Solid moat + moderate 3–5% growth | 15–20x |
| Average moat + low growth | 10–15x |
| Narrow moat + competitive threats | 8–12x |
| Cyclical / declining | < 8x |

**2. Simplified DCF (clear growth trajectory).** Present value of owner earnings over ~10 years plus terminal value. Discount rate: risk-free rate + 3–5% company risk premium. If the result swings wildly with a 1% discount-rate change, the valuation is unreliable and needs a larger margin of safety.

**3. Historical earnings-power method (cyclical or quick estimates).** Average earnings over 7–10 years, never just the latest year, at a conservative 10–15x.

## Margin of Safety

Purchase price = intrinsic value × 60–70%. Margin of safety is not "cheap": a low P/E is not safety — what matters is the gap between price and true value. A company with a narrowing moat has no margin of safety even at 8x earnings.

Required discount scales with uncertainty: highly certain excellent businesses 20–30%; generally excellent 30–40%; uncertain 40–50%; if intrinsic value cannot be reliably estimated, do not invest.

Price determines return: a too-high purchase price can undo a decade of favorable business developments. Focus on earnings quality, not the multiple itself; a 25x P/E can be reasonable for a franchise and seriously overvalued for a cyclical.

## Capital Allocation: Five Paths and the One-Dollar Test

Five paths in priority order: reinvest in the existing business (incremental return above cost of capital); acquire whole businesses (fair price non-negotiable); buy minority stakes below intrinsic value; repurchase stock (only when price is clearly below intrinsic value); pay dividends (when nothing better exists — and say so honestly).

**One-dollar test:** every dollar retained should create at least one dollar of market value. Consistently below 1:1 → return the money. Distinguish restricted earnings (needed just to stay competitive) from unrestricted earnings (the only truly discretionary portion).

**Buyback discipline:** the only legitimate reason is price below intrinsic value. Correct behavior: buybacks accelerate when the price falls, not when it rises. Buybacks to "offset option dilution" or signal "confidence" harm continuing shareholders.

## Book Value and P/E

Book value is a quick reference, not a valuation basis — it diverges from economic reality (historical-cost carry, unrecorded float value, above-book buybacks). P/E means nothing in isolation: interpret against the rate environment, never across industries, and always with earnings quality in view.
